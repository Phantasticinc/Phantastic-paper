## A stock is a slice of a company

When a company sells **shares**, each share is a small slice of ownership. If a company has 1,000,000 shares and you hold 100, you own 0.01% of it. You do not own its buildings or products directly. You own a claim on its future profits, which is why the price moves when people change their minds about those profits.

Shares can pay a **dividend** (a share of profits paid in cash), but many companies pay none. Nothing is guaranteed: a stock price can fall to zero.

## An ETF is a basket you trade like a stock

An **exchange-traded fund (ETF)** holds many investments at once, such as hundreds of stocks, and itself trades on an exchange all day like a single stock. Many ETFs follow an **index**, a published list of companies, for example the 500 largest US companies.

- One purchase gives you a slice of many companies.
- Its price moves with the basket, so one company's bad day matters less.
- It still falls when the whole market falls. A basket is not a shield.

## The fee: expense ratio

Funds charge a yearly fee called the **expense ratio**, taken from the fund's assets automatically. A 0.10% expense ratio costs about $10 per year on $10,000. Over decades small fees add up, so compare them.

## Stock or ETF?

Neither is "better". A single stock concentrates risk in one business; an ETF spreads it. Later lessons on position sizing and diversification show how to think about that.

## Key terms

- **Share**: one unit of ownership.
- **Ticker**: the short code, like a name tag, used to find a security.
- **Index**: a published list of securities used as a benchmark.
- **Expense ratio**: the yearly fund fee, as a percent of what you hold.
